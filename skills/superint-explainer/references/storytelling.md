# Storytelling, evidence and teaching

A good explainer updates a plausible but incomplete picture of the world. The viewer leaves able to repeat one sentence and remember one image.

## The editorial question

Not "is the topic interesting?" but: **which concrete expectation of the viewer can a verifiable explanation change?** If research yields no fresh, surprising and true angle, change the angle. Never invent controversy, and never make a story interesting by removing its caveats.

**The message comes first.** When the brief states a message or the request takes a position, that is the film's angle, and the editorial question becomes: which true, concrete reasons make it convincing to a viewer who doubts it? Give the strongest reasons, answer the strongest objection fairly in a line, and keep every caveat that changes the conclusion. Never trade the message for a different question that seems more surprising. If the evidence does not support the message, tell the user before the script.

Reject or change the angle if:

- the key claim exists only in unverified secondary sources;
- the surprise works only by hiding an essential condition;
- the "common belief" is merely put into the viewer's mouth;
- a single case, a model or an opinion cannot carry the promised generality.

## Evidence rules

- Use primary sources where possible (papers, agencies, official data) and check in the original what was actually measured.
- Keep survey year, publication year and access date apart.
- Keep correlation, causation, plausible explanation and opinion apart.
- Numbers carry unit, baseline, period and honest rounding. Comparisons use compatible system boundaries. Model calculations name their assumptions and a sensible range.
- Several sites repeating one claim are not independent confirmation.
- Never cite a source you could not read. Never claim an expert review that did not happen.

## Teaching design

- **The one sentence** the viewer must be able to repeat afterwards.
- **The misconception** most viewers hold, and the moment the film breaks it.
- **The aha** and the exact image that triggers it.
- Show what a system *does* before naming it. Exception: easily confused terms get defined early.
- Concrete before abstract; one new relationship at a time.
- **Metaphors must preserve relationships, not just looks:** selection, feedback, transport, limit, competition, delay. For every load-bearing metaphor note what it maps correctly, which false property the viewer might carry over, and where the film translates it back into the real mechanism.
- **Personification** may illustrate behavior. It must never silently add intent, experience or consciousness as fact. Check this especially for AI, evolution, the brain and the immune system.

## Narrative architectures

Choose from the evidence and the question, not from a template. Combine where it helps; never force a number of acts, twists, analogies or jokes.

| Architecture | Flow | Fits when | Fails when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mechanism as plot | trigger → reaction → limit → next reaction → result | a process is explained causally | personification replaces the real mechanism |
| Conditional reversal | expectation → contradiction → model → conditions → new evaluation | a surprise can be cleanly justified | the condition vanishes from the conclusion |
| Scaling thought experiment | assumptions → first simulation → limit → bigger simulation → consequence | orders of magnitude are the real topic | assumptions look like natural constants |
| Personal quantification | everyday life → graspable measure → changed reference frame → decision | finiteness or distribution should be felt | an example model looks like individual fate |
| Feedback loop with lever | experience → mechanism → amplification → possible interruption → limits | a pattern can be explained and partly changed | a complex problem shrinks to willpower |
| Systemic trade-off | simple fix → side conditions → trade-offs → levels of action | several interests or systems interact | complexity becomes an excuse for vagueness |
| Chronological case | event → background → decisions → escalation → finding → evaluation | the sequence itself matters | fact, reconstruction and opinion blur |

**Section function test.** Every section must do at least one job: sharpen the main question; set up a prerequisite for the next explanation; show a cause or mechanism; answer the most reasonable objection; reveal a consequence or limit; update the opening model. If two sections can swap places without losing meaning, the order is arbitrary: rework it. Before each major transition ask what an attentive viewer would object to right now, and answer the strongest objection, never a strawman.

## Hook, title and ending

Develop them together; title and thumbnail form one promise, an incomplete relationship the film resolves.

- The opening makes the question understandable early: an observation, an event, a familiar experience or an explicitly hypothetical scenario. No greeting, logo or agenda. In 9:16 the hook lands within 2 s.
- Don't hold answers back artificially. Give an answer and let its consequence raise the next question; viewers stay for progress in understanding.
- The ending states the message in one sentence over its own image and reframes the opening, so the first image means something new. Then an end card holds 3–6 s while the music resolves; a film never stops on its last word.
- No mandatory hopeful ending and no motivational speech. A limit, an open question or a serious consequence can be the right ending.

## Language for the ear

- Verbs carry the mechanism: "the signal reaches the next cell; it starts copying itself", not "downstream processes are activated".
- Mix sentence lengths on purpose: short sentences for consequences and turns, longer ones for conditions and comparisons.
- Translate numbers: unit, baseline, period and a comparison that answers the actual question. Know whether each is measured, estimated, simplified or a scenario.
- Hedges are part of the claim ("can", "in this study", "so far"); keep them audible. Distinguish impossible, unlikely, unknown and impractical.
- Humor comes from the subject or a shift in scale; it never hides a definition and never punches at those affected. No joke quota.
- "You" assigns a perspective or scenario; "we" marks a shared inquiry. Never prop up a claim with a vague "we know".
- No filler ("let's dive in", "in today's video"). On-screen text never repeats the narration.
- Write natively in the narration language; never translate English sentence structure into German. In German, watch long compounds and ambiguous pronouns.

## Script revision passes

After choosing the best of three drafts and grafting in the strongest lines of the others:

1. **Teacher pass:** run the section function test. Is every term placed in the model before it is used? Does every metaphor have its limit?
2. **Ear pass:** read it aloud. Is every thought clear on first hearing? Are references and pronouns unambiguous?
3. **Cut-and-truth pass:** remove every word that doesn't pull its weight, then ask which cuts turned a limited finding into a general claim: "in some studies" → "science shows", a population finding → a personal certainty, a possible explanation → a confirmed cause, a simplified calculation → a precise prediction. Also check that numbers and units survived unchanged and that local additions have their own sources.

## Rubrics

Use them to find defects; a number without a named defect is worthless.

**Research and script** (weights 30/25/20/15/10; one central factual error cannot be offset by anything else): evidence and scope; explanation and dramaturgy; clarity by ear; title promise kept; originality and tone. For the script also: spoken rhythm and the ending.

**Concept:** fit to topic; is every picture understood without explanation (the real thing, no symbol world); a few strong worlds with one art direction and smooth travel between them; originality; visual richness; memorability.

**Beats:** visual flow; the picture develops every 2–4 s inside a world; worlds change only with the argument, no channel-surfing; clarity of each beat; does the aha land; does the ending land the message.

**Frames:** shape quality; color harmony; light consistency; depth; charm; readability at thumbnail size.
